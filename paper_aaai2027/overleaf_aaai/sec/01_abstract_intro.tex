\begin{abstract}
An expert demonstration is the one input to imitation learning whose cost does
not fall when compute is bought or a simulator is downloaded, so under a fixed
allowance the only lever left on policy performance is what each demonstration
contains. Robot-gated query-efficient interactive imitation learning reduces to
one template: score a visited state, compare against a threshold, hand control to
the expert at the first crossing. That template decides when to call the expert
and fixes the other two decisions, which failure to correct and where the
demonstration begins, by default. We present \textbf{DISEIL}
(\textbf{D}emonstration d\textbf{I}stillation for \textbf{S}ample-\textbf{E}fficient
\textbf{I}mitation \textbf{L}earning), which takes the flag as given and decides
the other two. Each round it partitions the failures into modes over a
six-dimensional geometric descriptor of the step where the policy first becomes
unreliable, targets the mode of highest mean peak loss among the largest, and
prescribes the configuration of a demonstration not yet collected, verified
against a constraint graph of what the environment permits before any expert time
is spent. Across five tasks under two observation modalities at a budget of twenty
demonstrations, against the query-efficient DAgger family and a uniform-random
allocation control, DISEIL attains the best or joint-best mean held-out success
rate in every setting, by a mean of 2.80 points over the strongest baseline in
each.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Behaviour cloning fits a policy to an expert's actions on the states the expert
visits \cite{pomerleau1988alvinn,bain1995cloning}, and its failure mode is
covariate shift: the learner's own actions carry it off the demonstrated support,
where errors compound \cite{shimodaira2000covariate,ross2011dagger}. Interactive
imitation learning removes the compounding by aggregating corrections on the
states the learner visits \cite{ross2011dagger,celemin2022iil}, and pays for it in
expert effort, the cost that binds
\cite{mandlekar2018roboturk,khazatsky2024droid}. Performance scales with the
coverage of a policy's demonstrations, while those added within a covered setting
saturate \cite{lin2024datascaling}, so once an allowance of $B$ demonstrations is
fixed, the content of each is all that is left to choose.

The query-efficient members of the DAgger family gate the expert behind a
per-state predicate on action discrepancy \cite{zhang2017safedagger},
predictive uncertainty from dropout \cite{menda2017dropoutdagger} or an ensemble
\cite{menda2019ensembledagger}, novelty and risk \cite{hoque2021thriftydagger},
or a diffusion policy's own per-step denoising loss
\cite{lee2025diffdagger,chi2023diffusionpolicy}. They differ in the scalar and
share the template. An acquisition has three parts, when to call the expert, which
of the round's failures to spend the call on, and where the corrective
demonstration begins. The template computes the first and takes the other two by
default: the failure corrected is whichever rollout tripped the gate first, and
the demonstration begins in the state that rollout was already in. Under an
unbounded budget the two defaults cost nothing, since every mode is corrected
eventually. Under a budget of twenty demonstrations they are the whole problem.

Neither missing decision can be recovered from a scalar attached to one state. It
carries no representation in which two failures are the same mistake and a third
is a different one, so a gate corrects the recurring mode as often as the
recurrence throws it up while a rare mode waits. It also inherits the state that
tripped it, frequently one the policy has already ruined, so the expert
demonstrates recovery from a corruption instead of avoidance of it. The failures
of a round are not independent events but a small set with structure, and that
structure is recoverable from geometry: a descriptor of the configuration at the
step where the policy first becomes unreliable partitions them into geometrically
distinct groups. Whether those groups are worth allocating over is an empirical
question, and the ablations answer it.

DISEIL takes the flag as given and computes the other two parts of the
acquisition. Each round it anchors every failure at the first threshold crossing
rather than at the peak, partitions the failures into modes over that descriptor,
and targets the near-dominant mode of highest mean peak loss. It then prescribes
the configuration of a demonstration not yet collected, verified against a
constraint graph of what the task permits. The round's failure set is distilled to
one demonstration, chosen so that what the expert supplies stands for the mode
rather than for one episode.

We contribute three things. We separate the acquisition rule into when, which and
where, and show the DAgger family fixes the last two by default. We present
DISEIL, which attains the best or joint-best mean held-out success rate in all ten
settings at a budget of twenty demonstrations, by a mean of 2.80 points over the
strongest baseline in each. And we locate the advantage in allocation rather than
in per-demonstration informativeness: removing the mode structure costs a mean
4.37 points of success while information gain does not fall, which is what a
redundantly spread budget looks like.
