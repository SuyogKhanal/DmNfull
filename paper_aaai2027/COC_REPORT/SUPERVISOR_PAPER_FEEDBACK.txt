The load-bearing principles (these drive most of the edits)
1. Frame everything as a general framework; specific numbers are just one instance. This is his single most repeated point. A framework is general — you take one instance and run it. B=20, D=1 demo/round, the 5 tasks, the specific thresholds — those are one configuration you tested, not the framework itself. If you write your contribution as "works at a 20-demonstration budget," a reviewer reads it as "only works at 20." Instead: "works under any fixed/restricted budget," and B=20 is the instance you validated. Same logic applies to the algorithm, the questions, and any hardcoded component. Rule of thumb he gave: use the symbol in the method/algorithm, give the concrete value only in Setup.
2. The Method section must contain only your novelty. Anything standard — the BC objective, dataset aggregation, the generic query-gate template, the silhouette criterion, A*/BFS, even the Diff-DAgger rule — is not yours. Citing it inside "Method" doesn't fix it; listing it under the Method heading still signals ownership, and reviewers skim. His exact warning: a reviewer reading fast sees standard equations under your Method and concludes "half of this is copied, where's the novelty?" So common material goes up to Related Work / a Mathematical Background, or is explicitly framed as "we follow standard practice" / "for completeness." His analogies: you use integration without explaining who invented it; you mention SVM as a classifier you use, not as if you're deriving it.
3. Abstract everything swappable. Don't hardcode a specific technique as if the method depends on it. His example: don't say "K-means," say "a clustering step C, instantiated here as agglomerative clustering (could also be K-means, etc.)." That way the framework "involves clustering," not "requires K-means." Note this one is easy for you — you already use agglomerative, so you just present it as one instantiation of a generic clustering step. The tradeoff he flagged: more generality invites "did you ablate the alternatives?" You do the top ablations only and defer the rest to reviewer requests.
4. Read your own paper adversarially. His framing: read it as if a stranger wrote it, criticize every word, "assume this guy is a bullshit guy." Reading it as the author (who knows the intent) is why you didn't catch that you use "mode" in two conflicting senses.
5. Space discipline. You're at the ceiling and every line counts (see the page-limit note at the end). This is why he's hunting dangling words and fat captions.

Page limit, Limitations, and the supplementary workflow

The page rule: 7 pages of content + up to 2 pages of references. Your question — "if my references are under 2 pages, can I spill into content?" — the answer is no. If the venue meant "9 total including references" they'd have said so; "7 + 2" means content is capped at 7 regardless of how short your references are. This is why all the space-saving matters.
Limitations — check whether AAAI requires it in the main paper. If not explicitly required, consider moving it (or part of it) to the supplementary to buy space.
Point to the supplementary for extra experiments. Before the Conclusion (or right after Q2/Q3), add a one-liner: "Additional experiments in supplementary A.1 / A.2 / A.3." That line does double duty — it lets you offload results, and it tells a future reader (arXiv, your GitHub, or the AAAI-hosted supplementary if accepted) that an appendix exists and where to look. AAAI hosts supplementary as a separate file; they don't merge it into the main PDF.
The workflow he wants during revision: keep the appendix in the same file as the main paper while you and your supervisors are reviewing, so cross-references resolve and, more importantly, so they can see everything and tell you what to pull up into the main paper or push down. You split it only at submission: trim the main paper to the 9-page limit, submit the appendix as a separate supplementary file.
