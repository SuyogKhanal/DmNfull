# Part A — Per-Paper Cards

### ross2011dagger
- **Title:** A Reduction of Imitation Learning and Structured Prediction to No-Regret Online Learning
- **Authors:** Stéphane Ross, Geoffrey J. Gordon, J. Andrew Bagnell
- **Venue+Year:** AISTATS 2011
- **Summary:** Sequential prediction problems such as imitation learning violate the i.i.d. assumption of statistical learning because the learner's own predictions determine future observations, causing errors to compound (up to T² mistakes over a T-step horizon). The paper proposes DAGGER (Dataset Aggregation), an iterative meta-algorithm that trains a stationary deterministic policy and is analyzed as a no-regret online learning algorithm, yielding performance that grows linearly in the horizon T. At each iteration DAGGER runs the current policy to collect the states it actually visits, queries the expert for the correct actions at those states, aggregates them into a growing dataset, and retrains. The method is simple, reuses existing supervised learners as a black box, and is demonstrated on two imitation-learning tasks plus a benchmark sequence-labeling problem.
- **Core method/idea:** Iteratively roll out the current learned policy, query the expert for correct actions on the states the learner visits, aggregate all such (state, expert-action) pairs across iterations into one dataset, and retrain a single deterministic policy—reducing imitation learning to no-regret online learning.
- **Category:** baseline
- **Key results/claims:** On Super Tux Kart (average falls per lap over 20 iterations), DAGGER obtained a policy that "never falls off the track after 15 iterations" and after only 5 iterations "almost never falls off the track," significantly outperforming SMILe (β=0.1) and the supervised baseline, which still "falls off the track about twice per lap on average." On Super Mario Bros., performance is measured as average distance travelled per stage (scale up to ~3000), with DAGGER outperforming SMILe and supervised baselines. On handwriting recognition, the non-structured SVM baseline achieves 82% character accuracy while DAGGER reaches roughly 0.85 character accuracy on the test folds.
- **Relation to PACE:** DAGGER is the foundational interactive imitation-learning algorithm that PACE builds on and directly compares against, but whereas DAGGER queries the expert at every state the learner visits, PACE instead uses a VLM/LLM to perceive rollout failures and prescribe targeted corrective scenarios from which new expert demos are collected.

### zhang2017safedagger
- **Title:** Query-Efficient Imitation Learning for End-to-End Autonomous Driving
- **Authors:** Jiakai Zhang, Kyunghyun Cho
- **Venue+Year:** AAAI 2017
- **Summary:** The paper addresses imitation learning for end-to-end autonomous driving, where a primary policy maps front-facing camera images to driving actions (steering angle and braking). It notes that DAgger improves over plain supervised learning but requires constantly querying an expensive reference policy. To reduce this cost, the authors propose SafeDAgger, which introduces a learned safety policy that predicts whether the primary policy's action will deviate significantly from the reference policy, and uses it to select only a small subset of collected states for expert labeling. Experiments in the TORCS car-racing simulator show SafeDAgger needs far fewer reference-policy queries while converging faster, which the authors attribute to an automated curriculum-learning effect.
- **Core method/idea:** A binary "safety policy" is trained to predict where the primary policy will make large errors (without querying the expert); it is used both to select a small subset of states for DAgger data collection and, at test time, to switch control between the primary and reference policies, yielding a query-efficient DAgger variant.
- **Category:** baseline
- **Key results/claims:** After the supervised-learning stage, 77.70% of training examples are deemed "safe" at threshold tau = 0.0025 (so ~20% are considered unsafe). The safety policy switches to the reference policy for only 7.11% (without traffic) and 10.81% (with traffic) of driving time during test, with a clear downward trend across iterations. SafeDAgger collects 30k/30k/10k labeled examples across its three iterations, versus the original DAgger uniformly subsampling the same amounts from up to 390k collected examples per iteration; after the third iteration the SafeDAgger-trained primary policy is reported as "perfect" on the completed-laps metric, progressing faster than DAgger on both average laps and damage-per-lap.
- **Relation to PACE:** SafeDAgger is a direct interactive-imitation-learning baseline for PACE: it reduces expert queries via a learned safety/uncertainty gate over states, whereas PACE instead uses a VLM to perceive rollout failures and an LLM to prescribe targeted corrective reset states for expert demonstrations.

### menda2017dropoutdagger
- **Title:** DropoutDAgger: A Bayesian Approach to Safe Imitation Learning
- **Authors:** Kunal Menda, Katherine Driggs-Campbell, Mykel J. Kochenderfer
- **Venue+Year:** arXiv 2017 (preprint)
- **Summary:** The paper presents a probabilistic extension to DAgger for safe imitation learning that considers the novice policy's distribution over actions for a given observation. The novice is trained with dropout as a Bayesian neural network, so querying it N times yields a distribution over actions whose entropy reflects the novice's confidence/uncertainty at a state. A decision rule lets the novice act only when a sufficient probability mass of its sampled actions lies within a ball of radius tau around the expert's action; otherwise control is handed back to the expert. The method is evaluated on MuJoCo HalfCheetah-v1 and a Dubins Car Lidar driving domain, showing improved safety over other DAgger variants and classic imitation learning while matching their learning performance.
- **Core method/idea:** Use Monte-Carlo dropout to obtain a Bayesian estimate of the novice policy's action distribution, and let the novice act only if the estimated probability that its action falls within radius tau of the expert action exceeds a threshold p (parameters tau, p, dropout probability d); this jointly enforces low novice entropy and closeness to the expert, generalizing SafeDAgger* (recovered when d=0).
- **Category:** baseline
- **Key results/claims:** On MuJoCo HalfCheetah-v1 (averaged over 50 episodes), DropoutDAgger makes "no compromise to the safety of the combined system" while learning a well-performing novice as quickly as other DAgger variants; with added observation noise (sigma = 0.1), all variants except DropoutDAgger and BehaviorCloning have their safety compromised, yet DropoutDAgger retains comparable learning performance. On the Dubins Car Lidar domain under high aleatoric uncertainty, DropoutDAgger "has best learning and safety performance," maintaining perfect safety while BehaviorCloning and SafeDAgger* keep safety but exhibit worse learning. Hyperparameters used: HalfCheetah DropoutDAgger tau=0.3, p=0.6, dropout d=0.05 (SafeDAgger* tau=0.6); Vanilla DAgger beta_0=1, beta_i=0.63*beta_{i-1}.
- **Relation to PACE:** DropoutDAgger is a direct DAgger-family baseline for PACE: it decides *whether to query the expert* at uncertain states via a Bayesian-dropout confidence measure, whereas PACE instead uses a VLM to perceive rollout failures and an LLM to prescribe targeted corrective reset states for a diffusion-policy learner.

### menda2019ensembledagger
- **Title:** EnsembleDAgger: A Bayesian Approach to Safe Imitation Learning
- **Authors:** Kunal Menda, Katherine Driggs-Campbell, Mykel J. Kochenderfer
- **Venue+Year:** IROS 2019
- **Summary:** The paper presents EnsembleDAgger, a probabilistic (Bayesian) extension of DAgger for safe imitation learning that aims to maximize the novice policy's share of actions while constraining the probability of failure. It approximates a Gaussian Process using an ensemble of neural networks, and uses the ensemble variance as a measure of the novice's confidence (its "doubt"). A decision rule combines this doubt with the SafeDAgger-style discrepancy between novice and expert actions to decide when it is safe to let the novice act versus hand control to the expert. The authors evaluate on an inverted pendulum and the MuJoCo HalfCheetah environment, reporting improved safety and learning performance over other DAgger variants and classic imitation learning.
- **Core method/idea:** Quantify novice uncertainty via an ensemble-of-neural-networks GP approximation and use that variance ("doubt"), together with a bounded action-discrepancy condition, as a safety decision rule that decides when the expert must intervene, so the expert acts in unfamiliar/uncertain states and the novice acts in familiar ones.
- **Category:** baseline
- **Key results/claims:** On HalfCheetah, the doubt rule's Pareto frontier dominates the discrepancy rule (achieves better novice performance for the same combined-system compromise), and the full EnsembleDAgger rule is strictly more conservative than either component, always improving combined-system performance. On the inverted pendulum, the doubt-rule instance with chi = 10^-3 shows superior learning performance and "exhibits no failures in any of the six epochs in any of the repeated experiments" (30 repetitions), whereas neither discrepancy-rule instance (tau = 10^-1, tau = 5*10^-2) is failure-free.
- **Relation to PACE:** EnsembleDAgger is a direct interactive-imitation-learning baseline for PACE: it decides expert intervention from ensemble-based uncertainty at individual states, whereas PACE instead perceives rollout failures with a VLM and prescribes targeted corrective scenarios rather than querying the expert at uncertain states.

### hoque2021thriftydagger
- **Title:** ThriftyDAgger: Budget-Aware Novelty and Risk Gating for Interactive Imitation Learning
- **Authors:** Ryan Hoque, Ashwin Balakrishna, Ellen Novoseller, Albert Wilcox, Daniel S. Brown, Ken Goldberg
- **Venue+Year:** CoRL 2021
- **Summary:** ThriftyDAgger is a robot-gated interactive imitation learning algorithm that actively queries a human supervisor given a desired budget of interventions. It uses a learned switching policy to solicit interventions only at states that are sufficiently (1) novel, where the robot policy has no reference behavior to imitate, or (2) risky, where the robot has low confidence in task completion; to detect the latter the paper introduces a new metric for estimating risk (probability of task success) under the current robot policy. Unlike prior robot-gated methods (SafeDAgger, LazyDAgger) that require careful parameter tuning, ThriftyDAgger only requires the supervisor to specify a target context-switching rate and sets thresholds automatically. It is evaluated in simulated peg insertion (Robosuite), a physical da Vinci cable-routing task, and a three-robot fleet user study (N=10).
- **Core method/idea:** Combine a novelty measure (out-of-distribution detection) with a learned risk metric that estimates probability of task success, using both jointly in a budget-aware switching policy to decide when to request human interventions during both training and execution.
- **Category:** baseline
- **Key results/claims:** On simulated Robosuite peg insertion, ThriftyDAgger reaches a 73/100 autonomous success rate (vs. 57/100 for HG-DAgger, 48/100 for LazyDAgger, 24/100 for SafeDAgger and Behavior Cloning) and a 100% (20/20) intervention-aided success rate; ablations dropping novelty or risk fall to 49/100. On the physical cable-routing task it achieves 12/15 autonomous and 15/15 (100%) intervention-aided success. In the fleet user study, the authors report ThriftyDAgger "increases human and robot performance by 58% and 80% respectively compared to the next best algorithm while reducing supervisor burden" (throughput 9.2 vs 5.1, Concentration pairs 33.0 vs 20.9, 7.9 interventions vs 10.0).
- **Relation to PACE:** ThriftyDAgger is a direct DAgger-family baseline for PACE: it queries the expert at uncertain (novel/risky) states via a learned gate, whereas PACE instead has a VLM perceive rollout failures and an LLM prescribe targeted corrective reset states from which new demos are collected.

### hoque2021lazydagger
- **Title:** LazyDAgger: Reducing Context Switching in Interactive Imitation Learning
- **Authors:** Ryan Hoque, Ashwin Balakrishna, Carl Putterman, Michael Luo, Daniel S. Brown, Daniel Seita, Brijen Thananjeyan, Ellen Novoseller, Ken Goldberg
- **Venue+Year:** IEEE CASE 2021
- **Summary:** LazyDAgger is an interactive imitation learning algorithm that extends SafeDAgger to reduce the burden of corrective human interventions by minimizing context switches between supervisor and autonomous control. It learns a meta-controller that estimates the discrepancy between the learner and supervisor and cedes control when the learner's actions are predicted to deviate significantly. Compared to SafeDAgger, it (1) uses asymmetric switching criteria to favor sustained rather than fragmented interventions and (2) injects noise into supervisor actions to widen the visited-state distribution and improve robustness to covariate shift. Notably, the learned meta-controller can also be applied at execution time (LazyDAgger-Execution) to improve safety and reliability with minimal switching.
- **Core method/idea:** Learn a discrepancy classifier / meta-controller with asymmetric switch-to-supervisor and return-to-autonomous thresholds, plus supervisor-action noise injection, so that human interventions are longer, contiguous, and more informative while total context switches are reduced.
- **Category:** baseline
- **Key results/claims:** "LazyDAgger can reduce context switches by an average of 60% over SafeDAgger on 3 continuous control tasks while maintaining state-of-the-art policy performance." Across the 3 simulated continuous-control tasks it "achieves task performance on par with DAgger with 88% fewer supervisor actions than DAgger and 60% fewer context switches than SafeDAgger." In physical fabric-manipulation experiments with an ABB YuMi robot, at execution time LazyDAgger "reduces context switches by 60% while achieving a 60% higher success rate than SafeDAgger."
- **Relation to PACE:** LazyDAgger is a direct interactive-IL baseline family member (a SafeDAgger extension) that decides *when* to query the expert via learner-supervisor discrepancy, whereas PACE instead uses a VLM to perceive rollout failures and an LLM to prescribe targeted corrective reset states for a diffusion-policy learner.

### kelly2019hgdagger
- **Title:** HG-DAgger: Interactive Imitation Learning with Human Experts
- **Authors:** Michael Kelly, Chelsea Sidrane, Katherine Driggs-Campbell, Mykel J. Kochenderfer
- **Venue+Year:** ICRA 2019
- **Summary:** The paper proposes HG-DAgger (Human-Gated DAgger), a variant of DAgger designed for interactive imitation learning from human experts on real-world systems. Rather than switching control stochastically at each timestep (as in DAgger's robot-centric sampling), the human expert acts as a gating function: they take exclusive, uninterrupted control whenever the novice policy enters an unsafe region and hand control back when the state is safe, with expert action labels collected only during these recovery/intervention trajectories. In addition to training the novice policy, HG-DAgger learns a safety threshold for a model-uncertainty ("doubt") risk metric derived from a neural-network ensemble, computed as the mean doubt over the final 25% of recorded human-intervention points. The method is evaluated on a simulated and real-world autonomous driving task (weaving between stationary obstacle cars on a two-lane road) against DAgger and behavioral cloning.
- **Core method/idea:** A human expert directly controls the gating function, taking full control to demonstrate corrective/recovery behavior when the novice acts unsafely, and the system learns a doubt-based risk threshold from these interventions rather than tuning DAgger's beta or picking a threshold a priori.
- **Category:** baseline
- **Key results/claims:** HG-DAgger showed faster and more stable learning than DAgger and behavioral cloning in sample efficiency, road departure rate, and collision rate. When novice rollouts were initialized inside the estimated permissible set P-hat vs. outside, the mean collision rate was 12x lower and the mean road departure rate was 20x lower (Table I: 0.607e-3 vs. 7.533e-3 collision rate; 0.607e-3 vs. 12.092e-3 road departure rate), and road-departure duration was less than half. On the real test vehicle (first 5,000 samples, Table II), HG-DAgger had 0 collisions and 0 road departures vs. 1 collision each for BC and DAgger and 6 vs. 1 road departures; its steering-angle distribution was 21.1% closer to human driving data (Bhattacharyya distance 0.0834 vs. DAgger 0.1057 and BC 0.1173), indicating more human-like behavior.
- **Relation to PACE:** HG-DAgger is a direct interactive-imitation-learning baseline in the DAgger family that PACE contrasts with, using expert-triggered interventions at unsafe states instead of PACE's VLM-perceived, clustered, and LLM-prescribed corrective scenarios for targeted demo collection.

### laskey2017dart
- **Title:** DART: Noise Injection for Robust Imitation Learning
- **Authors:** Michael Laskey, Jonathan Lee, Roy Fox, Anca Dragan, Ken Goldberg
- **Venue+Year:** CoRL 2017
- **Summary:** The paper addresses covariate shift in imitation learning, where a behavior-cloned policy drifts to unseen states because it is trained only on the supervisor's on-distribution demonstrations. Rather than using an on-policy approach like DAgger (which queries the supervisor at robot-visited states, is tedious for humans, computationally expensive, and can visit dangerous states), the authors keep learning off-policy but inject optimized noise into the supervisor's control stream during data collection. This forces the supervisor to demonstrate recovery behavior at the boundary of the ideal policy, so the collected demonstrations better match the state distribution the trained robot will encounter. They validate on four simulated MuJoCo locomotion tasks (Walker, Hopper, Half-Cheetah, Humanoid) with an algorithmic supervisor and on a real Toyota HSR grasping-in-clutter task with four human supervisors.
- **Core method/idea:** DART (Disturbances for Augmenting Robot Trajectories) is a noise-injection variant of Behavior Cloning that iteratively fits a parameterized noise distribution to approximate the trained robot's expected error, injecting that optimized noise into the supervisor's demonstrations to reduce covariate shift without on-policy retraining.
- **Category:** baseline
- **Key results/claims:** On Humanoid, DART is "3x faster in computation time" and during training "only decreases the supervisor's cumulative reward by 5%, whereas DAgger executes policies that have over 80% less cumulative reward than the supervisor." On the real-robot grasping-in-clutter task, DART (alpha=3) achieves a 79% success rate versus Behavior Cloning's 49% (DART alpha=6 reaches 72%); the abstract summarizes this as "on average a 62% performance increase over Behavior Cloning." DART achieves parity with DAgger across all four MuJoCo domains.
- **Relation to PACE:** DART is a direct point of contrast for PACE's DAgger-family baselines: both aim to combat covariate shift by exposing the learner to corrective/recovery states, but DART does so via optimized noise injection into expert demos rather than PACE's VLM-perceived, clustered, and LLM-prescribed targeted corrective reset states.

### ross2014aggrevate
- **Title:** Reinforcement and Imitation Learning via Interactive No-Regret Learning
- **Authors:** Stéphane Ross, J. Andrew Bagnell
- **Venue+Year:** arXiv 2014 (preprint)
- **Summary:** The paper extends interactive, no-regret imitation-learning approaches (like DAgger) so they can exploit cost-to-go information rather than only agreement with the expert's chosen actions. It introduces AggreVaTe (Aggregate Values to Imitate), which at each iteration rolls out the current learner's policy, interrupts at a random time step to explore an action, then hands control back to the expert to obtain a cost-to-go estimate; these cost-weighted examples are aggregated across iterations and fed to a no-regret online cost-sensitive learner. The authors further extend the idea to reinforcement learning via an approximate-policy-iteration variant they call NRPI (No-Regret Policy Iteration). The contribution is primarily theoretical, providing regret-style performance guarantees and a unifying view showing that prior methods (e.g., DAgger, SEARN) are special cases of this general no-regret strategy.
- **Core method/idea:** AggreVaTe generalizes DAgger by learning to minimize the expert's cost-to-go (not just imitation loss), casting the sequence of policies as an online cost-sensitive classification problem solvable by any no-regret online learner, with the RL extension NRPI.
- **Category:** baseline
- **Key results/claims:** This is a theoretical paper and reports no empirical experiments or numeric results in the main text; its central claims are performance/regret bounds — e.g., it shows AggreVaTe provides a statistical-regret guarantee (stronger than DAgger's statistical error-reduction bound) and that NRPI matches guarantees "as strong as any available in the literature" for approximate policy iteration, while noting cost-to-go estimation is impractical when TQ_max is O(T^2) unless the state distribution is close to the exploration distribution.
- **Relation to PACE:** AggreVaTe is a foundational DAgger-family interactive imitation-learning method in the same lineage as PACE's DAgger-variant baselines, but it expands the expert query at random/exploratory states using cost-to-go, whereas PACE instead uses a VLM/LLM to perceive failures and prescribe targeted corrective scenarios rather than querying at uncertain states.

### sun2017aggrevated
- **Title:** Deeply AggreVaTeD: Differentiable Imitation Learning for Sequential Prediction
- **Authors:** Wen Sun, Arun Venkatraman, Geoffrey J. Gordon, Byron Boots, J. Andrew Bagnell
- **Venue+Year:** ICML 2017
- **Summary:** This paper introduces AggreVaTeD, a differentiable, policy-gradient extension of the AggreVaTe imitation-learning algorithm (Ross & Bagnell, 2014) designed to work with deep neural network policies (both feedforward and recurrent). It assumes access to a near-optimal cost-to-go oracle (expert) at training time and leverages it to learn faster and with less data than less-informed deep reinforcement learning. The authors provide a theoretical analysis showing imitation learning with AggreVaTeD can enjoy up to exponentially lower sample complexity than RL, and demonstrate the method on dependency parsing from raw images and on high-dimensional robotics control tasks. They also show the learner can exceed sub-optimal experts.
- **Core method/idea:** Reduce sequential prediction to interactive imitation learning by treating the predictor as a policy and running stochastic gradient descent on the AggreVaTe objective, querying an optimal or near-optimal cost-to-go oracle at rolled-out states to compute descent directions for deep (including recurrent) policies.
- **Category:** baseline
- **Key results/claims:** Natural-gradient AggreVaTeD surpasses the expert by 5.8% on Acrobot swing-up and 25% on Cart-pole, and outperforms the expert by 5.4% on the MuJoCo Walker while reaching 97% of the expert's performance on Hopper; theory shows up to exponentially lower sample complexity than RL.
- **Relation to PACE:** AggreVaTeD is a DAgger-family interactive imitation-learning baseline that queries an expert oracle at visited states, whereas PACE replaces oracle querying with VLM-perceived rollout-failure clustering and LLM-prescribed corrective scenarios to train a diffusion policy.

### pomerleau1988alvinn
- **Title:** ALVINN: An Autonomous Land Vehicle in a Neural Network
- **Authors:** Dean A. Pomerleau
- **Venue+Year:** NeurIPS 1988
- **Summary:** ALVINN is a 3-layer back-propagation neural network trained for the task of road following. It takes images from a video camera and a laser range finder as input (a 30x32 video retina, an 8x32 range-finder retina, and a road-intensity feedback unit feeding 29 hidden units) and outputs, over 45 direction units, the turn curvature the vehicle should travel to stay on the road. Because collecting real road images under many conditions is logistically difficult, the network is trained on 1200 artificially generated ("simulated") road snapshots spanning varied orientations, positions, lighting, and noise. Trained networks were tested on the NAVLAB, CMU's autonomous navigation test vehicle, and shown to follow real roads under certain field conditions.
- **Core method/idea:** Cast autonomous road following as supervised learning: a connectionist network is trained via back-propagation on labeled (simulated) road images to directly map raw sensor input to steering direction, rather than using fixed hand-engineered image-processing pipelines.
- **Category:** background
- **Key results/claims:** "After 40 epochs of training on the 1200 simulated road snapshots, the network correctly dictates a tum [turn] curvature within two units of the correct answer approximately 90% of the time on novel simulated road images." On hardware, "the network can accurately drive the NAVLAB at a speed of 1/2 meter per second along a 400 meter path through a wooded area of the CMU campus under sunny fall conditions," comparable to the best traditional vision-based CMU navigation algorithm under the limited conditions tested. Removing the range-finder input (constant obstacle-free range image) caused "no noticeable degradation in driving performance."
- **Relation to PACE:** ALVINN is the seminal end-to-end imitation-learning-for-control system that trains a policy to mimic expert steering from demonstrations, making it a foundational ancestor of the behavioral-cloning learners and DAgger-family interactive imitation baselines that PACE builds upon and improves via VLM/LLM-guided corrective data collection.

### pomerleau1991efficient
- **Title:** Efficient Training of Artificial Neural Networks for Autonomous Navigation
- **Authors:** Dean A. Pomerleau
- **Venue+Year:** Neural Computation 1991
- **Summary:** This paper presents the ALVINN (Autonomous Land Vehicle In a Neural Network) system, a single-hidden-layer backpropagation network (30x32 = 960-unit input retina, 5 hidden units, 30 output units) that learns to steer the CMU Navlab van by imitating a human driver. It introduces "training on-the-fly," in which the network is trained in real time on the live camera image paired with the human's current steering direction, addressing prior drawbacks of training on synthetic road images. To overcome the lack of off-center recovery examples and overtraining on repetitive straight-road inputs, each original image is laterally shifted into 14 additional exemplars with correspondingly adjusted steering labels, and a buffer of ~200 recent scenes is maintained with a low-error/random replacement policy. The trained network generalizes across single-lane paved/unpaved roads and multilane lined/unlined roads.
- **Core method/idea:** Real-time behavioral cloning of a human driver ("training on-the-fly"), where laterally shifted synthetic views of each live image supply the off-center recovery examples that naive imitation of a center-driving expert would never provide.
- **Category:** baseline
- **Key results/claims:** ALVINN learns to drive "in under 5 minutes"; training requires ~50 iterations of the digitize-replace-train cycle on a Sun-4 (~5 min while a person drives ~4 mph); each image is shifted 7 times left and 7 times right in 0.25-m increments to create 15 exemplars, replacing the 10 lowest-error plus 5 random of ~200 scenes; the network processes 25 images per second and drives at up to the Navlab's maximum of 20 mph ("over twice as fast as any other sensor-based autonomous system has driven the Navlab"). The original synthetic-image scheme by contrast needed ~6 hr of Sun-4 CPU to generate scenes plus 45 min of training.
- **Relation to PACE:** ALVINN is a foundational imitation-learning/behavioral-cloning method whose hand-crafted image-shifting trick to inject off-center recovery data prefigures the covariate-shift/expert-recovery problem that DAgger-style interactive IL (and PACE's corrective-scenario generation) later address in a principled, data-driven way.

### celemin2022iil
- **Title:** Interactive Imitation Learning in Robotics: A Survey
- **Authors:** Carlos Celemin, Rodrigo Pérez-Dattari, Eugenio Chisari, Giovanni Franzese, Leandro de Souza Rosa, Ravi Prakash, Zlatan Ajanović, Marta Ferraz, Abhinav Valada, Jens Kober
- **Venue+Year:** Foundations and Trends in Robotics 2022
- **Summary:** This is a survey of Interactive Imitation Learning (IIL), the branch of imitation learning where a human provides feedback intermittently during robot execution to improve behavior online. The authors argue IIL is both data-efficient (human feedback guides the robot directly toward improved behavior rather than trial-and-error as in RL) and robust (feedback is provided over the learner's own trajectories, minimizing the teacher-learner distribution mismatch seen in offline methods like Behavioral Cloning). Observing that IIL terminology, structure, and applicability are not unified in the literature, the paper proposes a unified terminology and organizes methods by human-robot interaction (feedback types), interfaces, learning models/function approximators, user experience, applications, and benchmarks. It focuses on real-world robotic applications and discusses their implications, limitations, and open research questions.
- **Core method/idea:** A structuring survey that unifies IIL terminology and taxonomy, groups the field by feedback type, interface, learned model, data handling, relation to RL, and human factors, and reviews benchmarks, applications, and open challenges.
- **Category:** background
- **Key results/claims:** Not an empirical paper, so no experimental numbers are reported; the main quantitative artifact is Figure 1.1, a histogram of surveyed IIL papers published per year (spanning roughly 1991 through the beginning of 2022), showing rising adoption of interactive teaching strategies. The paper claims IIL yields richer datasets because "the distribution of data collected is induced by the learner instead of the teacher, avoiding data mismatch issues," and notes that benchmark adoption in IIL "has been limited" due to the human-in-the-loop factor.
- **Relation to PACE:** It provides the foundational IIL framing and taxonomy (including the DAgger-family on-policy correction paradigm) into which PACE's LLM/VLM-guided corrective-demonstration loop and its DAgger-variant baselines fit.

### mandlekar2020iwr
- **Title:** Human-in-the-Loop Imitation Learning using Remote Teleoperation
- **Authors:** Ajay Mandlekar, Danfei Xu, Roberto Martín-Martín, Yuke Zhu, Li Fei-Fei, Silvio Savarese
- **Venue+Year:** arXiv 2020 (preprint)
- **Summary:** The paper addresses covariate shift in imitation learning for 6-DoF robot manipulation, where offline-trained policies fail in "bottleneck" regions requiring precise action sequences and where DAgger-style offline relabeling is infeasible. The authors build a remote teleoperation data-collection system (extending RoboTurk) that lets a human monitor a policy via web-browser video stream and intervene using a smartphone to take over control through bottlenecks. They introduce Intervention Weighted Regression (IWR), which treats the human intervention timing as an implicit signal of where bottlenecks are and re-weights the dataset so that intervention samples are prioritized over on-policy samples. Experiments on contact-rich Threading and Coffee Machine tasks show IWR outperforms full-demonstration and other intervention-based baselines.
- **Core method/idea:** IWR aggregates both human-intervention samples and the robot's on-policy samples, then trains behavioral cloning while re-weighting the distribution (q(s,a) ∝ α·ρ_I + ρ_R, with α = |D_R|/|D_I| to sample the two sets in equal proportion) so that actions that traverse bottlenecks are reinforced.
- **Category:** inspiration:prioritize
- **Key results/claims:** On the single-operator Threading task, IWR reaches 87.3% final success vs. 76.7% for Full Demos, 75.3% for HG-DAgger, and 74.7% for IWR-NB (base policy 58.0%). On the multi-operator Coffee Machine task, IWR reaches an average final success of 87.5% (over 35% improvement over the base policy's 52.0%) vs. HG-DAgger 69.6% and Full Demos 64.9%. Cross-dataset comparisons (Tables III/IV) show IWR outperforms baselines even when trained on data the baselines collected, indicating the gains come from how IWR leverages data, not just data quality.
- **Relation to PACE:** IWR prioritizes human-intervention samples at policy-failure bottlenecks by re-weighting the training distribution, which parallels PACE's Prioritize stage of focusing corrective effort on failure regions—though IWR relies on real-time human interventions rather than VLM-perceived failure clustering and LLM-prescribed reset scenarios, and uses an LSTM-BC learner instead of a diffusion policy.

### liu2023sirius
- **Title:** Robot Learning on the Job: Human-in-the-Loop Autonomy and Learning During Deployment
- **Authors:** Huihan Liu, Soroush Nasiriany, Lance Zhang, Zhiyao Bao, Yuke Zhu
- **Venue+Year:** RSS 2023

### florensa2017reversecurriculum
- **Title:** Reverse Curriculum Generation for Reinforcement Learning
- **Authors:** Carlos Florensa, David Held, Markus Wulfmeier, Michael R. Zhang, Pieter Abbeel
- **Venue+Year:** CoRL 2017
- **Summary:** The paper tackles goal-oriented RL tasks whose natural reward is sparse, requiring only a single state in which the task is achieved as prior knowledge (no demonstrations or reward shaping). The robot is trained "in reverse," first learning to reach the goal from start states nearby it and then from increasingly distant start states. New start states are automatically generated by a short random (Brownian) walk from previous start states that received some but not full reward, producing a curriculum of start-state distributions that adapts to the agent's current performance. The method is demonstrated on simulated navigation (point-mass maze, ant maze) and fine-grained manipulation (ring-on-peg, key insertion) tasks in MuJoCo that state-of-the-art RL methods fail to solve.
- **Core method/idea:** Automatically generate an adaptive curriculum of initial/start states that grows outward from the goal, selecting "good starts" whose estimated return falls in an intermediate band (Rmin <= R <= Rmax) and expanding from them via Brownian motion, so training concentrates on tasks at the appropriate difficulty.
- **Category:** inspiration:prescribe
- **Key results/claims:** With plain Uniform Sampling (TRPO baseline) the probability of reaching the goal is "around 10% for the ring task and 2% for the key task," whereas the reverse-curriculum method succeeds from a wide range of far-away start states; learning curves are reported as mean and variance over 5 random seeds, and an "Oracle (rejection sampling)" upper bound and an asymmetric self-play comparison (Sukhbaatar et al.) further show the "good starts" heuristic outperforms both uniform and all-starts ablations.
- **Relation to PACE:** Like PACE's Prescribe step, it targets learning effort by generating reset/start states (sub-task entry points) at the right difficulty rather than querying the expert everywhere, and its "good starts" band-selection prefigures PACE's Prioritize step for choosing which corrective scenarios to collect demos from.

### eysenbach2018leavenotrace
- **Title:** Leave no Trace: Learning to Reset for Safe and Autonomous Reinforcement Learning
- **Authors:** Benjamin Eysenbach, Shixiang Gu, Julian Ibarz, Sergey Levine
- **Venue+Year:** ICLR 2018
- **Summary:** Real-world reinforcement learning requires repeatedly resetting the environment between attempts, which usually demands extensive manual human intervention and risks driving the agent into unrecoverable states. This paper proposes an autonomous method that simultaneously learns a forward (task) policy and a reset policy, where the reset policy returns the environment to the initial state for a subsequent attempt. By learning a value function for the reset policy, the method can detect when the forward policy is about to enter a non-reversible state and issue uncertainty-aware safety "early aborts." Experiments show this reduces the number of manual resets, reduces unsafe actions leading to irreversible states, and automatically induces a learning curriculum.
- **Core method/idea:** Jointly learn a forward policy and a value-based reset policy; use an ensemble of reset-policy Q-functions to trigger uncertainty-aware "early aborts" (optimistic/realist/pessimistic) that keep the agent in reversible states, requesting a hard manual reset only after N failed reset attempts.
- **Category:** uncertainty
- **Key results/claims:** In the gridworld didactic example, raising the early-abort threshold Qmin from 0 to 0.4 reduced hard resets by 78% without increasing steps to solve; on the pusher task the method matched status-quo reward with roughly half the hard resets, and raising Qmin to 80 achieved nearly the same reward with 33% fewer hard resets; on cliff cheetah it used an order of magnitude fewer hard resets with substantially higher final reward, and a threshold of 10 prevented 69% of the early aborts the status quo would have performed; with a single reset attempt the algorithm used 64% fewer hard resets than status quo on pusher, and increasing to 4 reset attempts gave a further 2.5x reduction in hard resets while decreasing reward by less than 25%.
- **Relation to PACE:** It shares PACE's use of ensemble-based uncertainty to decide when to intervene (analogous to the uncertainty-triggered querying in EnsembleDAgger/ThriftyDAgger baselines), but targets autonomous RL resets and safety rather than actively prescribing corrective expert-demonstration scenarios for an imitation-learning diffusion policy.

# Part B — Thematic Synthesis (Related Work)

*Prose is written to be adapted into a related-work section; every citation uses the citekeys in [references.bib](references.bib). Numbers and specific claims should be double-checked against the per-paper cards in Part A before use.*

### B.1 Interactive imitation learning and the DAgger family
Behavior cloning dates to ALVINN, which trained a neural network to map camera images directly to steering commands \cite{pomerleau1988alvinn,pomerleau1991efficient}, but naive cloning suffers from covariate shift: small action errors move the agent into states absent from the training data, and mistakes compound over the horizon. DAgger reframes imitation learning as no-regret online learning, aggregating expert labels on the states the *learner* actually visits and thereby bounding error linearly in the horizon \cite{ross2011dagger}, with AggreVaTe and Deeply AggreVaTeD extending the idea to cost-to-go objectives and differentiable deep policies \cite{ross2014aggrevate,sun2017aggrevated}. Because vanilla DAgger demands near-constant access to an expert, a family of "safe" and query-efficient variants gate *when* to defer to the expert: SafeDAgger learns a safety classifier over states \cite{zhang2017safedagger}, DropoutDAgger and EnsembleDAgger use Bayesian dropout and ensemble variance as the gating signal \cite{menda2017dropoutdagger,menda2019ensembledagger}, HG-DAgger hands control to a human expert on demand \cite{kelly2019hgdagger}, and DART instead injects optimized noise into demonstrations so that offline cloning is itself robust to covariate shift \cite{laskey2017dart}. A more recent thread reduces human burden and context-switching (LazyDAgger) and explicitly budgets interventions by novelty and risk (ThriftyDAgger) \cite{hoque2021lazydagger,hoque2021thriftydagger}, while intervention-weighted and deployment-time systems learn continually from targeted human takeovers \cite{mandlekar2020iwr,liu2023sirius}; the whole landscape is surveyed by Celemin et al. \cite{celemin2022iil}. PACE departs from this family by replacing per-state uncertainty/safety gating with VLM-perceived *failure analysis* and LLM-*prescribed* corrective scenarios, so expert effort is spent on curated failure modes rather than on every uncertain state.

### B.2 Diffusion policies and generative imitation learning
Denoising diffusion probabilistic models established a stable, expressive class of generative models \cite{ho2020ddpm}, which Diffuser adapted to trajectory-level planning \cite{janner2022diffuser} and Diffusion Policy adapted to visuomotor control, modeling multimodal action distributions and improving training stability over unimodal regression \cite{chi2023diffusionpolicy}. Implicit Behavioral Cloning captures similar multimodality through energy-based models and contributes the now-standard PushT manipulation task \cite{florence2021implicitbc}. Most directly related to our setting, Diff-DAgger derives an interactive-imitation query signal from the diffusion-policy training objective itself \cite{lee2025diffdagger}. PACE uses a diffusion policy as its learner and is complementary to Diff-DAgger: rather than triggering queries from model uncertainty, it triggers demonstration collection from observed, clustered failure modes.

### B.3 Uncertainty estimation for querying
The gating decisions in safe-DAgger variants rest on predictive uncertainty, most commonly Monte-Carlo dropout \cite{gal2016dropout} and deep ensembles \cite{lakshminarayanan2017ensembles}, which DropoutDAgger and EnsembleDAgger instantiate directly as safety signals \cite{menda2017dropoutdagger,menda2019ensembledagger}. PACE deliberately avoids calibrated action-uncertainty, using observed failure *outcomes* as its trigger instead of predictive variance.

### B.4 LLMs/VLMs for robot planning, code, and reward generation
A large body of recent work turns foundation models into sources of robot behavior: SayCan grounds LLM plans in learned affordances \cite{ahn2022saycan}, Inner Monologue closes the loop with textual environment feedback \cite{huang2022innermonologue}, and PaLM-E and RT-2 fuse perception with language into embodied and vision-language-action models \cite{driess2023palme,brohan2023rt2}. A complementary line has LLMs emit *executable structure*—robot policy code (Code as Policies, ProgPrompt) or 3D value maps (VoxPoser) \cite{liang2023codeaspolicies,singh2023progprompt,huang2023voxposer}. Closest to our Prescribe step, Eureka and Language-to-Rewards use LLMs to synthesize reward code and parameters \cite{ma2024eureka,yu2023language2rewards}. PACE adapts this generativity to a narrower target: specifying corrective *reset states* and sub-task entry points from which fresh expert demonstrations are collected, rather than whole plans or reward functions. In our implementation the prescribing LLM is Qwen3 \cite{yang2025qwen3}, whose unified thinking / non-thinking modes we treat as two experimental setups (reasoning enabled vs disabled).

### B.5 LLM/VLM failure reflection and self-correction
REFLECT summarizes multimodal robot experience into a hierarchical narrative to explain and correct task failures \cite{liu2023reflect}, and AHA is a VLM trained specifically to detect and reason over failures in robotic manipulation \cite{duan2025aha}; in the language domain, Reflexion and Self-Refine show that models can iteratively critique and improve their own outputs \cite{shinn2023reflexion,madaan2023selfrefine}. These works motivate PACE's Perceive step, in which a VLM describes *why* a rollout failed as the first stage of the pipeline; concretely, we use the Qwen3-VL vision-language model \cite{bai2025qwen3vl} to perceive and describe rollout failures.

### B.6 Active learning: coreset, diversity, and clustering
Pool-based active learning selects the most informative examples under a labeling budget \cite{settles2009active}, and modern diverse-batch methods inform our Partition and Prioritize steps: the core-set / k-center formulation selects points for max-min coverage of the feature space \cite{sener2018coreset}, BADGE combines predictive uncertainty and diversity through gradient embeddings \cite{ash2020badge}, and farthest-point sampling yields deterministic, uniform min-max coverage \cite{eldar1997fps}. PACE clusters rollout failures into modes using K-means clustering \cite{lloyd1982kmeans} and then applies farthest-point/coreset selection to pick a small, diverse, representative subset before prescribing corrections.

### B.7 Visual representations for manipulation
Pre-trained visual encoders supply the features PACE clusters over: R3M learns a reusable representation from egocentric video \cite{nair2022r3m}, MVP uses masked visual pre-training \cite{radosavovic2022mvp}, and VIP learns a value-implicit representation and dense visual reward \cite{ma2023vip}. We use R3M embeddings as the image descriptor for failure states.

### B.8 Simulators and benchmarks
Evaluation builds on the ManiSkill line of large-scale manipulation benchmarks \cite{mu2021maniskill,gu2023maniskill2,tao2024maniskill3} and on robomimic's systematic study of learning from offline human demonstrations \cite{mandlekar2021robomimic}, with the PushT task originating in Implicit BC \cite{florence2021implicitbc}; the robosuite framework supplies the Lift, Wipe, and Door tasks we run on a UR5/UR5e arm \cite{zhu2020robosuite}. Reset-state and curriculum ideas—reverse curriculum generation \cite{florensa2017reversecurriculum} and learning-to-reset for autonomous operation \cite{eysenbach2018leavenotrace}—inform how PACE chooses the entry states it prescribes.
