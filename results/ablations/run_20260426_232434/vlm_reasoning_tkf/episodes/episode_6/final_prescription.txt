Here are simple, practical answers you can use for guiding corrective demonstrations.

1) Where in the maze does the AI get confused?
- At the very start, when there is a dangerous cell right next to the agent. The safe options are to move away from that danger, but the agent sometimes moves toward the danger instead.
- In tight parts of the maze where there are several hazards nearby (like a corridor with fires on or near the path), the agent can get the idea that progress toward the goal justifies stepping toward danger. The decision point around hazards is where the confusion comes up.

2) What should a good walkthrough look like in that area?
- It should clearly show hazard avoidance from the first moment: do not move toward a nearby fire, even if it seems tempting.
- It should then demonstrate following a safe route that stays in areas with no immediate danger, making steady progress toward the goal.
- The walkthrough should be complete from the start to the goal, showing how to handle a few tricky hazard layouts without stepping into danger.
- If possible, include a short, plain explanation of why the hazard was avoided (so the reason for the safe choice is obvious).

3) What will the AI learn from seeing that walkthrough?
- It will learn to treat nearby danger as a strong reason to avoid certain moves, not just as a distant concern.
- It will learn to favor safe corridors or paths that keep away from hazards, rather than pushing straight for quick but risky progress.
- It will start to generalize this hazard-avoidance reasoning to other random mazes with similar situations, not just the exact numbers in this episode.

4) How many walkthroughs are needed and how should they vary?
- Do several high-quality demonstrations that cover different hazard setups (not just one layout).
- A good target is roughly 6–12 complete demonstrations, each showing hazard avoidance in a different but plausible arrangement.
- Vary the situations: different ways hazards appear near the start, different cluster patterns in the middle, and different distances to the goal. This helps the policy learn to react correctly in a variety of random mazes, not just one fixed case.
- Focus on variety rather than length: include different ways the same safe path can be taken, as long as the core lesson—avoid danger first and stay on a safe route—is clear.

If you keep these points in mind, you’ll collect corrective demonstrations that reinforce safe behavior and help the policy generalize to new, randomized mazes.
