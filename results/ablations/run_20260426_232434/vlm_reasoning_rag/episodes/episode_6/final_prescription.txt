Here are four simple answers you can use.

1) Where in the maze does the AI get confused?
- At the very first choice, there’s a danger right next to the start on the right. The AI tries to move toward the goal anyway and steps onto the fire, ending the run. The confusion is that a nearby hazard isn’t given enough weight when deciding the first move.

2) What should a good walkthrough look like in that area?
- Show that the agent notices the nearby fire and avoids it. Then demonstrate taking a safe route that stays away from all fires, using a clear, safe path along the outer edge, and continuing toward the goal. The emphasis is safety first, then progress, not rushing through just to get closer to the goal.

3) What will the AI learn from seeing that walkthrough?
- It will learn to treat hazards that are right next to the start as priorities to avoid. It will pick safe corridors when there are immediate dangers, even if that makes the route longer. This hazard-avoidant behavior should generalize to other mazes with random layouts.

4) How many walkthroughs are needed and how should they vary?
- Do about 4–6 demonstrations for this exact starting setup to solidify the pattern. Then add 2–3 more demonstrations where the hazard is placed in other directions around the start (to teach hazard handling in different spots). Include a couple of demonstrations with longer safe routes and slight variations in the safe path. The goal is to have enough variety so the lesson generalizes to many randomized mazes.
