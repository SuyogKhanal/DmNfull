Here are simple, human-friendly answers you can use.

1) Where in the maze does the AI get confused?
- It happens right at the starting spot. There’s a fire tile directly to the right, and there’s a safe path along the left edge. The AI ends up moving toward the fire instead of taking the safe edge route. This pattern can show up again in other random mazes, whenever a hazard sits next to the start but a safe edge path is available.

2) What should a good walkthrough look like in that area?
- Show, clearly, that when you spot a danger on the right, you choose the safe corridor on the left rather than stepping toward the danger.
- Then demonstrate following that safe edge route (the edge corridor) until you can head toward the goal from a safer position.
- Include a brief explanation of why the safe choice is better here (hazard avoidance takes precedence over rushing straight to the goal). The key idea is: always check for danger and use the safe corridor when a hazard is adjacent.

3) What will the AI learn from seeing that walkthrough?
- It will learn to put hazard awareness first: if there’s a dangerous tile next to you, don’t move toward it. Instead, use the safe corridor to bypass the hazard.
- It will learn to rely on corridor-based routing when hazards are near, and this behavior should carry over to other randomized mazes with similar layouts.

4) How many walkthroughs are needed and how should they vary?
- Aim for about five to eight corrective walkthroughs.
- Make sure they cover different near-start hazard placements and different safe corridors (left edge, top edge, right column) so the AI learns to pick the safest corridor in various layouts.
- Include at least one walkthrough that matches the exact failure setup but shows the hazard-avoidant path, plus several variations where the hazard sits on different sides of the start.
- Keep the mazes varied (since they’re randomized) to help the model generalize to new but similar situations.
