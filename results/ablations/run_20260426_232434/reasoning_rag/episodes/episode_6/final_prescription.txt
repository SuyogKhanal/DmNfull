Here are straightforward answers to your four questions, in simple language.

1) Where in the maze does the AI get confused?
- At the very start, right after you’re placed at the first decision point. There’s a fire immediately to the right, but the left edge is a safe path. The AI tends to push toward the goal anyway and ends up stepping into the fire because it’s not prioritizing safety when hazards are nearby.

2) What should a good walkthrough look like in that area?
- Show hazard awareness first: clearly point out that the tile on the right is dangerous and the left path is safe.
- Demonstrate choosing the safe route along the edge and then continuing along a longer, safe detour toward the goal, staying away from the hazards.
- Include simple notes like “safety first,” “avoid danger,” and “safe corridor ahead.”
- Add a few variations so the same idea shows up in different shapes: sometimes hazards appear on another side, or there are multiple hazards near the start. Each variation should still emphasize finding and sticking to a safe detour rather than rushing toward the goal.

3) What will the AI learn from seeing that walkthrough?
- It will learn to notice hazards that are near the starting point and to put safety before speed.
- It will learn to use safe corridors or detours along the edges to bypass hazards, instead of chasing the shortest path.
- It will generalize this hazard-awareness pattern to other mazes that change from episode to episode.

4) How many walkthroughs are needed and how should they vary?
- Do a modest batch, roughly 6–12 demonstrations, and mix in variety.
- Start with simple cases: hazards on one side with a clear safe corridor.
- Then add tougher variations: hazards on multiple nearby tiles, or a blocked direct route requiring a longer detour.
- Vary the maze layout and the positions of hazards so the pattern stays the same (hazard awareness + safe detour) but the exact setup changes.
- It helps to annotate each walkthrough with the hazard check (e.g., “hazard on the right; taking the safe corridor”) so the lesson sticks.
