Here are short, plain-English answers to your four questions.

1) Where in the maze does the AI get confused?
- Right at the very start. There’s a fire in the cell next to the starting point, and the AI makes the wrong first move toward that fire. So the confusion is about what to do when a hazard appears immediately in front of the first choice.

2) What should a good walkthrough look like in that area?
- In that start-area, show a safe, clear response: ignore the nearby fire and begin along a safe path toward the goal.
- Use a few different safe starting options so the agent sees more than one way to begin avoiding hazards.
- Include a simple explanation during the demonstration about why the hazard is avoided and why the safe detour is the right idea.
- Make sure the demonstrations work in different mazes, since the layout changes every episode. The goal is to teach the same safe behavior even when other fires appear elsewhere.

3) What will the AI learn from seeing that walkthrough?
- It will learn that when a hazard is right in front of the first move, the right move is to avoid it and take a safe detour toward the goal.
- It will start to connect hazard cues with avoidance, rather than ignoring them.
- It will generalize this safer behavior to new mazes where hazards show up near the start or in other similar early positions.

4) How many walkthroughs are needed and how should they vary?
- Start with a small set of core examples: about three strong demonstrations that begin with the start-area hazard and show safe detours to the goal.
- Add a few variations (about two to three more) where fires appear in different places along the path, still starting with the hazard near the start, so the agent learns the same safe approach in different layouts.
- In total, aim for three to six demonstrations. The goal is to cover different hazard placements while keeping the same lesson: avoid hazards near the start and use safe detours to reach the goal.
